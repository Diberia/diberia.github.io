Diberian Languages

Grammar of Diberian

Word Order

Diberian uses Subject-Object-Verb word order for ordinary sentences, [Example: "I an apple eat"]
Object-Subject-Verb word order for most questions, [Example: "An apple I eat?"]
And Verb-Subject-Object word order for "what" questions and imperative sentences. [Example: "What eat I ...?", "Eat an apple!"]

Diberian usually places adjectives behind the nouns. For example, if you want to say "A nice tasty apple", you would say "An apple nice tasty".

Articles

Diberian uses no articles whatsoever (Yippee :))

Nouns and noun classes

Diberian nouns are treated as two seperate classes:
- Hard Nouns (Example: "R²in" -> "Rhine" (the river))
- Soft Nouns (Example: "Hvama" -> "Mother")

These noun classes reflect on Determiners like "This" and on the genetive/possessive forms of Pronouns. (See the dictionary for more info)

Example:
- "Hrawu aćþrä" (This house [Hard noun])
- "Hćrev hvama" (This mother [Soft noun])

- "Jéra aćþrä" (My house [Hard noun])
- "Héra hvama" (My mother [Soft noun])

Adjectives

There is nothing much to say about them.
Only that they come after the nouns.

Root verbs, auxiliary verbs and verb classes

Unlike many other languages, Diberian has three Verb classes, THE auxiliary verbs and three verb time cases.
Let's begin!

"Root Verbs:"
Root verbs are the most basic forms of the verb (infinitive). Similar to english "To ...".
Alone, they can also signal a passive or subordinate use of a verb, as in "I eat, to be good".
Which would correspond to "ñgé adun giregan, ul² nit". Literally: "I eat, good to be", remember the "SOV" word order!

"Verb classes:"
Verb classes are mainly about "What else does this verb need?". There are three classes:
- Class I -> Standalone verbs
- Class II -> Auxiliary-requiring verbs
- Class III -> Help-requiring verbs

Class I can still use "Nit" (to be) for the other past forms, which itself cannot be.
Example: "Vamehiyn" -> "I knew"; "Vamehiyn nit..." -> "I have known..."

(Seems easy enough doesn't it?)
Class I includes verbs like "To have" ("þu"). These verbs are conjugated themselves for both Pronouns and Time cases, they do not require anything else.
Class II includes the most verbs, like "To bind" ("Lotgen"). These verbs require the auxiliary verb [More to them later] and are only conjugated for the time cases.
Class III includes verbs like "To stack" ("Stabe"). These verbs require both the auxiliary verb and the helper particle "ari". They are also only conjugated for time cases.

"Auxiliary verbs:"
The auxiliary verbs are simply conjugated versions of "To be" ("Nit"). Those are conjugated for:
- Pronouns
- Affections
- Subordinates
- Time cases

(See the dictionary for excact conjugations.)
Basically "To be" acts as Class I verb, when used for self-identification, as in "I am good" -> "ñgé ul² igan";
And as auxiliary verb, when paired with actual actions as in "I bind it" -> "ñgé härð lotgen igan".

When the verb is actively affecting the object, the auxiliary verb changes. Compare "ñgé härð lotgen igan" (I bind it) to "ñgé härð adun giregan" (I eat it),
where "igan" (1st per. Sg. Unaffected. Direct. Present) changes to "giregan" (1st. per. Sg. Affected. Direct. Present.).

The rule of thumb is basically: "Who? Does it affect the object? Is it a subordinate clause? When does it happen?".

"Verb time cases:"
There are five time cases in total. Yet only three, for a single verb.:
- Simple Present -> "I walk" [Main Verb: Present & Auxiliary Verb: Present]
- Continuous Past -> "I was walking" [Main Verb: Present & Auxiliary Verb: Past]
- Simple Past -> "I walked" [Main Verb: Past & Auxiliary Verb: Past]
- Present Perfect -> "I have walked" [Main Verb: Past & Auxiliary Verb: Present]
- Simple Future -> "I will walk" [Main Verb: Future & Auxiliary Verb: Present]

You create those time forms, by merging the main verb case with the auxiliary verb case, as demonstrated above.
The main verb time cases are:
- Present
- Past
- Future

There are also abstract concepts possible as in [Main Verb: Future & Auxiliary Verb: Past].
IMPORTANT: "I will have walked" is not a diberian time case officially... however, as seen before you can construct it with
[Main Verb: Future & Auxiliary Verb: Past]

Grammatical cases

Diberian has a lot of cases, because cases are mostly formed with suffixes. This list may catch some of them. The list counts: "13" cases.

- Nominative
- Accusative (Pronouns only)
- Dative (Formed with "-an" suffix plus accusative for Pronouns. Pronouns only)
- Local Genetive (uses "-rent" suffix for Nouns and Pronouns) [Of]
- Possessive Genetive (uses "-ren" suffix or simply the possessive form for pronouns)
- Benefactive (usually "-sjad" suffix plus also accusative for pronouns) [For]
- Inessive (usually "-an" suffix OR "a" particle plus normal form of pronouns for pronouns) [In, On]
- Ablative (usually "-tić" suffix plus normal form of pronouns for pronouns) [From]
- Allative (usually with the particle "a" in front and "-an" suffix) [Where to: "to"]
- Comitative (usually "-reihin" suffix plus accusative for pronouns) [With]
- Conditional I (usually "-sæn" suffix for the last verb. Verb only) [When]
- Conditional II (usually "fa-" prefix for the last verb. Verb only) [If]
- Locative (usually "-bhyn" suffix) [At]

Negation

Diberian's negation is quite straightforward. It uses "Ek" (Meaning: "No"). For basic negation as in "No!" and "Ina" for sentence negation
as in: "I am not" -> "Ina (ñgé) igan". Important to know is that the negation particle "Ina" comes before all other words.
Except for conjunctions: "..., that I am not good" -> "..., druð ina (ñgé) ul² awyn".

To build negative words like "Nothing" or "Never", Diberian places "Ek" in front of the positive word, as in:
"Ek orlw" (Meaning: "Nothing" or "No-one"; literally: "No everything").

"Can" and "multiple-verbs-sentences"

In Diberian, the Class I verb "To be able to" or simply "Can", is placed behind all other verbs in a sentence.
For example: "ñgé sure ihugjäþ igan ðésaht" (Meaning: "I can see you"). As we can see "ðésaht" ("I can") simply attaches
to the end of the sentence and does not force the infinitive of the verb "to See".

Things change once we throw sentences like "I am running to see you" into Diberian. Which translates to:
"ñgé ñgoa sure ihugjäþ" (ñgoa (Meaning: "I run") is a Class I verb and therefore needs no auxiliary verb by itself).
As we can now observe, the verb "to See" is now in it's infinite form
(Which corresponds to the Main verb in it's present conjugation for Class II and III verbs, and simply the 3rd person singular, or infinitive of Class I verbs)

About suffixes and prefixes

Diberian uses most of it's suffixes and prefixes to indicate grammatical cases, number or questions.
For example: Most Nouns use the "-ać" suffix for the plural.

Then there is the nominalizing suffix "-tað", which is used to turn any root verb into a Noun.
And the adjectivizing suffix "-ywð", which turns any root verb into an Adjective.

Etymology and historical development

The proposed Proto-Language

Proto-Paleo-European is the proposed ancestor language including languages such as Diberian, Basque, Iberian,
Etruscan, Raetic, Lemnian and even Proto-Indo-European. It is believed to be spoken around ~35.000 BCE until ~25.000 BCE.
It is supposed to be descended by an unknown primal language or simply gibberish.

Old Diberian and Proto-Vasconic

These two proposed ancestral languages would have been spoken around ~25.000 BCE to maybe ~7.000 BCE for Proto-Vasconic
and ~7.000 BCE to ~3.500 BCE for Old Diberian.

Where this version of reconstruction stands now

The current reconstructed form of Diberian is proposed to have been developed somewhere in ~3.500 BCE, although
this far back, the language would have been more of an old dialect of this one, whereas this version might have developed from
~3.500 BCE to maybe ~0 CE. Because small languages and communities like this, changed very slowly.

Dialects and regional varieties

Nydiska as a unification of dialects

Since diberian probably would've had a speaker group, which was all over Germany and Scandinavia, Diberian must have
developed dialects. The biggest one is even categorized as a whole different language; "Nydiska" (Norwegian for "New Diberian").

Nydiska emerged in Scandinavia by speakers adapting Germanic vocabulary and making the language far more easy.
For example: Nydiska misses almost 10 cases, complex sounds and changed to Subject-Verb-Object word order.
Furthermore: it misses the distinction between "Hard" and "Soft" nouns and the nominalizing and adjectivizing suffixes.

Usage

Formal and informal

As if these cases where not yet enough, Diberian also differentiates between formal and informal sentences.
These changes do not affect any meanings. They are purely about formality.
The formal and informal sentences are usually only build around direct subject pronouns as in "I eat".
Because diberian would already include all neccessary information inside the object and verb, it uses the pronoun only as
a formal particle. But songtexts, News and more are usually written in formal (standard) Diberian. The informal use is purely
for close friends, family or partners.

"ñgé ul² igan" -> "I am fine" (formal)
"Ul² igan" -> "'Am fine" (informal)

The most famous idiom in Diberian

If you are using the Affected auxiliary verb instead of the normal Unaffected auxiliary verb for sentences as "I am",
you get something on the line as:
- Gje iregan (From: Gire + giregan) -> "I can change" (Idiomatic formulation)
- Tje uresa (From: Sure + suresa) -> "You can change yourself"
- Fje ärðat (From: Farð + farðat) -> "He/She can change him/herself"
- Gjo urrad (From: Gor + gorrad) -> "We can change ourselves"
- Sje ecþe (From: Svecd + svecþé) -> "You all can change yourselves"
- Hja iðæ (From: Haiðe + haiðæ) -> "They can change themselves"

It can also be used by pairing as in "Sure giregan" -> "I would like to be able to change/affect you" (In an idiomatic expression).

Relations

Diberian

Diberian (endonym: ðyska) is a speculative reconstructed language, which is proposed to be spoken by the Diberians.
Diberians relatives are fairly unknown, yet the reconstruction would propose that Diberian is a relative of Basque.
Furthermore it is related to Etruscan, Raetic and Iberians.

The reconstruction proposes that Basque, Iberian, Etruscan and the Raetic language all share a larger "Pre-Indo-European" language family.
More realistically: It proposes that Basque and the Tyrsenian language family, share a common ancestor.
Iberian is a tough one, but it is often included in the proposed pre-Indo-European language family.

It is interesting to notice that reconstructed Proto-Indo-European would also fit this "Pre-Indo-European" language family, therefore I would propose that
Proto-Indo-European itself descended from a so called Proto-"Pre-Indo-European" language which would then also correspond as a predecessor for proposed Isolates like Basque

Nydiska

Nydiska is simply a descendand of Diberian, which adopted Germanic vocabulary and underwent significant simplifications in Grammar.
There is nothing much more to say about it...

Shanti

Shanti is a Uralic language of it's own proposed branch. It is completely based on Uralic languages like Finnish, Estonian, Hungarian but also
Khanty. It is proposed that the Diberian people adopted this language as a religious or ritualic language as they
expanded east towards the Ural Mountains. Yet it has underwent grammatical changes
as it was adopted by the Diberians.
