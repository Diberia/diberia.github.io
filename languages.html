Diberian Languages

Diberian, Proto-Vasconic and Proto-Paleo-European are not to be confused with conlangs.
They are speculatively reconstructed languages based on real evidence.
Shanti on the other hand leans almost entirely into being a conlang. I proposed this language as a ritualistic language for the Diberian culture,
because I assume that some might have moved eastwards, therefore adopting a Uralic language, which itself is already an assumption.

Grammar of Diberian

Word Order

Diberian uses Subject-Object-Verb word order in ordinary sentences, [Example: "I an apple eat"]
Object-Subject-Verb word order in most questions, [Example: "An apple I eat?"]
Verb-Subject-Object word order for "what" questions and imperative sentences. [Example: "What eat I ...?", "Eat an apple!"]

Diberian usually places adjectives behind the nouns. For example, to say "A nice tasty apple", you would say "An apple nice tasty".

Articles

Diberian uses no articles whatsoever (Yippee :))

Nouns and noun classes

Diberian nouns are treated as two separate classes:
- Hard Nouns (Example: "R²in" -> "Rhine" (the river))
- Soft Nouns (Example: "Hvama" -> "Mother")

These noun classes are reflected in determiners like "This" and on the genitive/possessive forms of pronouns. (See the dictionary for more information)

Example:
- "Hrawu aćþrä" (This house [Hard noun])
- "Hćrev hvama" (This mother [Soft noun])

- "Jéra aćþrä" (My house [Hard noun])
- "Héra hvama" (My mother [Soft noun])

Adjectives

There isn't much to say about them.
They simply come after nouns.

Root verbs, auxiliary verbs and verb classes

Unlike many other languages, Diberian has three verb classes, the auxiliary verbs and three verb time cases.
Let's begin!

"Root Verbs:"
Root verbs are the most basic forms of the verb (infinitive). Similar to English "to ...".
On their own, they can also indicate passive or subordinate uses of a verb, as in "I eat, to be good".
This corresponds to "ñgé adun giregan, ul² iran". Literally: "I eat, good to be", remember the "SOV" word order!

"Verb classes:"
Verb classes are mainly about "What else does this verb need?". There are three classes:
- Class I -> Standalone verbs
- Class II -> Auxiliary-requiring verbs
- Class III -> Help-requiring verbs

Class I can still use "Iran" (to be) for other past forms, which itself cannot be.
Example: "Vamehiyn" -> "I knew"; "Vamehiyn eðjur..." -> "I have known..."

(Seems easy enough, doesn't it?)
Class I includes verbs such as "To have" ("þu"). These verbs are conjugated themselves for both pronouns and time cases.
Class II includes the most verbs, like "To bind" ("Lotgen"). These verbs require an auxiliary verb [More to them later] and are only conjugated for the time cases.
Class III includes verbs like "To stack" ("Stabe"). These verbs require both an auxiliary verb and the auxiliary particle "ari". They are also only conjugated for time cases.

"Auxiliary verbs:"
There are four auxiliary verb groups:
- Identificational
- Stational
- Variable
- Subject-Object-agreeing suffixes (for transistive sentences only)

(See the dictionary for exact conjugations.)
Identificational auxiliary verbs indicate a persons identity. For example, compare:
- "(ñgé) John igan" -> "(I) am John"
with
- "(ñgé) ul² naður" -> "(I) am good/fine"

As you perhaps notice, the "Identificational" group is used mostly for Nouns while the "Stational" group is used mostly for adjectives and indicates
a state rather than an identification.

The "Stational" group is also used to conjugate other verbs. For example "Vamehiyn eðjur" (I have known) uses the stational "eðjur" instead of the identificational "igan", because "I know" represents a state someone is in.

Basically "To be" acts as a Class I verb, when used for self-identification, as in "I am good" -> "(ñgé) ul² naður";
And as auxiliary verb, when paired with actual actions as in "I bind it" -> "ñgéik härð lotgen eðjurdut".

When the verb is directly affecting the object, the auxiliary verb changes. Compare "ñgéik härð lotgen eðjurdut" (I bind it) with "ñgéik härð adun giregandut" (I eat it),
where "eðjur" (1st pers. sg. Unaffected. Direct. Present. Stational) changes to "giregan" (1st. per. Sg. Affected. Direct. Present.).

IMPORTANT: The variable auxiliaries are also the verb "To become"!

Now, to compare:
- Igan (Identificational as in "I am john")
- Eðjur (Stational and actual auxiliary verb for Class II and III verbs "I know")
- Giregan (Affected and actual auxiliary verb for Class II and III verbs, which change the object "I eat")
- Eðjurdut / Giregandut (Stational and Affected auxiliary verbs with the Subject-Object-agreeing suffix "-dut". Used in transistive sentences "I know it / I eat it")

The Subject-Object-agreeing suffixes for the auxiliary verbs are, for example:
- [Possessive genitive + reflexive "furva"] (I do something to myself)
- -tut (I do something to you (singular "you"))
- -dut (I do something to it)
- [Possessive genitive of "we" + reflexive "furva"] (I do something to us)
- -ustet (I do something to you (all))
- -itut (I do something to them)

These suffixes are used only in transistive sentences, which means that the sentence must include at least one object. Class I verbs also need these suffixes in transistive sentence as in "ðauhatut" (I have you). This is because Diberian hates pronouns when they are not needed. (Further information under "Usage")

"Verb time cases:"
There are five time cases in total. Yet only three, for a single verb.:
- Simple Present -> "I walk" [Main Verb: Present & Auxiliary Verb: Present]
- Continuous Past -> "I was walking" [Main Verb: Present & Auxiliary Verb: Past]
- Simple Past -> "I walked" [Main Verb: Past & Auxiliary Verb: Past]
- Present Perfect -> "I have walked" [Main Verb: Past & Auxiliary Verb: Present]
- Simple Future -> "I will walk" [Main Verb: Future & Auxiliary Verb: Present]

You create those time forms, by combining the main verb case with the auxiliary verb case, as demonstrated above.
The main verb time cases are:
- Present
- Past
- Future

There are also abstract concepts possible as in [Main Verb: Future & Auxiliary Verb: Past].
IMPORTANT: "I will have walked" is not an official Diberian time case... however, as seen before you can construct it with
[Main Verb: Future & Auxiliary Verb: Past]

You might notice that the reflexive agreements are missing. That is because Diberian uses "furva":
"Jéra furva adun giregandut" -> "I am eating myself"

You take the 3rd per. sg. receiving suffix, corresponding to the "doing" subject and form the "possessive genitive" with the reflexive pronoun "furva" into the subject.

BE CAREFULL THOUGH: Diberian loves to soften these suffixes. For example "Naðurdut" becomes "Naðurðut" for better pronounciation!

Grammatical cases

Diberian has a lot of cases, because cases are mostly formed with suffixes. Diberian contains 20 cases.

- Nominative
- Accusative (Pronouns only)
- Dative (Formed with "-an" suffix plus accusative for Pronouns. Pronouns only)
- Local Genitive (uses "-rent" suffix for Nouns and Pronouns) [Of]
- Possessive Genitive (uses "-ren" suffix or simply the possessive form for pronouns)
- Benefactive (usually "-sjad" suffix plus the accusative for pronouns) [For]
- Inessive (usually "-an" suffix OR the prefix "a-", plus normal form of pronouns, if it forms a pronoun) [In, On]
- Ablative (usually "-tić" suffix plus the normal form of pronouns, it it forms a pronoun) [From]
- Allative (usually with the prefix "a-" in front and "-an" suffix) [Where to: "to"]
- Comitative (usually "-reihin" suffix plus accusative for pronouns) [With]
- Conditional I (usually "-sæn" suffix for the last verb. Verb only) [When]
- Conditional II (usually "fa-" prefix for the last verb. Verb only) [If]
- Locative (usually "-bhyn" suffix, and possessive genitive for pronouns (always hard)) [At]
- Instrumental (usually "-vrus" for hard nouns such as pronouns (in the accusative) and "-ðrus" for soft nouns) [By, about]
- Motivative (usually "-gćatić" suffix plus accusative for pronouns) [Because of]
- Terminative (usually "-reinoæ" suffix plus accusative for pronouns) [Up to, Until, As far as]
- Prolative (usually "-tcad" suffix plus accusative for pronouns) [As, In the role of (e.g. a friend)]
- Ergative (Subject marker; usually "-ak, -ek, -ik, -ok or -uk" suffix depending on last vowel plus nominative for pronouns)
- Absolutive (Object marker; usually "-a, -e, -i, -o or -u" suffix depending on the last vowel. Not for pronouns!)
- Perlative (usually "-sehr²" suffix plus accusative for pronouns) [Through]

Negation

Diberian's negation is quite straightforward. It uses "Ek" (Meaning: "No"), For basic negation as in "No!", and "Ina" for sentence negation
as in: "I am not" -> "Ina (ñgé) igan". It is important to know that the negation particle "Ina" comes before all other words.
Except for conjunctions: "..., that I am not good" -> "..., druð ina (ñgé) ul² naðr".

To form negative words like "Nothing" or "Never", Diberian places "Ek" in front of the positive word, as in:
"Ek orlw" (Meaning: "Nothing" or "no one"; literally: "no everything").

"Can" and "multiple-verbs-sentences"

In Diberian, the Class I verb "To be able to", or simply "Can", is placed behind all other verbs in a sentence.
For example: "ñgé sure ihugjäþ igan ðésaht" (Meaning: "I can see you"). As we can see "ðésaht" ("I can") simply attaches
to the end of the sentence and does not force the infinitive of the verb "to See".

Things change once we introduce sentences such as "I am running to see you" into Diberian. Which translates to:
"ñgé ñgoa, sure ihugjäþ" (ñgoa (Meaning: "I run") is a Class I verb and therefore needs no auxiliary verb by itself).
As we can now observe, the verb "to See" is now in its infinite form
(Which corresponds to the main verb in its present conjugated form for Class II and III verbs, and simply the 3rd person singular, or infinitive of Class I verbs)

About suffixes and prefixes

Diberian uses most of its suffixes and prefixes to indicate grammatical cases, number, or questions.
For example: Most Nouns use the "-ać" suffix for the plural.

Then there is the nominalizing suffix "-tað", which is used to turn any verb root into a noun.
And the adjectivizing suffix "-ywð", which turns any verb root into an adjective.

Notes on Nydiska

In Nydiska, adjectives now come before nouns.

Etymology and historical development

The proposed Proto-Language

Proto-Paleo-European is the proposed ancestor language, which includes languages such as Diberian, Basque, Iberian,
Etruscan, Raetic, Lemnian and even Proto-Indo-European. It is believed to have been spoken around ~43.000 BCE until ~35.000 BCE.
It is supposed to be descended from an unknown primal language or a common ancestor, together with Proto-Indo-European perhaps.

Proto-Vasconic and Proto-Northern-Vasconic

These two proposed ancestral languages would have been spoken approximately ~35.000 BCE to ~25.000 BCE for Proto-Vasconic
and ~25.000 BCE to ~7.000 BCE for Proto-Northern-Vasconic.

Old Diberian

Old Diberian is much closer to this version of Diberian. I propose that it has been spoken aroun ~7.000BCE to ~3.500BCE

Where this version of reconstruction stands now

The current reconstructed form of Diberian is proposed to have developed around ~3.500 BCE, although
at that time, the language would have been more of an old dialect of this one, whereas this version might have developed from
~3.500 BCE to maybe ~0 CE. Small languages and communities like this, changed very slowly.

Dialects and regional varieties

Nydiska as a unification of dialects

Since Diberian probably would've had a speaker community, which was spread across Germany and Scandinavia, Diberian must have
developed dialects. The largest of these is even categorized as a separate language; "Nydiska" (Norwegian for "New Diberian").

Nydiska emerged in Scandinavia by speakers adapting Germanic vocabulary and making the language far more easy.
For example: Nydiska misses almost 18 cases, complex sounds and changed to Subject-Verb-Object word order.
Furthermore, it misses the distinction between "Hard" and "Soft" nouns and the nominal and adjectival-forming suffixes.

Usage

Overall Formality and Shortcuts

Generally, you would only use Nydiska for friends and family. It is after all,
the "unification" of all Diberian dialects.

Formal and informal (in Diberian)

As if these cases where not yet enough, Diberian also differentiates between formal and informal sentences.
These changes do not affect any meanings. They are purely about formality.
The formal and informal sentences are usually only built around direct subject pronouns as in "I eat".
Because diberian would already include all necessary information inside the object and verb, it uses the pronoun only as
a formal particle. Mostly only important literature is written in formal Diberian.

"ñgé ul² naður" -> "I am fine" (formal)
"Ul² naður" -> "'Am fine" (informal)

Also, there is a little difference in pronunciation when it comes to spoken Diberian:

"Ina adun giregan" -> "I am not eating" (written (technically informal))
"Inadun girgan" -> "I am not eating" (spoken (also technically informal, but this plays no role here))

Formal and informal (in Nydiska)

Nydiska is a bit harder because now one has to distinguish between academic Nydiska and casual Nydiska:

"Äkki eg kreyr im" -> "I am not creating (something)" (Academic, fully written, "book-like" version)
"Ekreyrim" -> "I am not creating (something)" (Casual and spoken version)

Nydiska is tricky to be written casually because it not only drops the pronoun, but also combines auxiliary and lexical verbs.

"Eg gott im" -> "I am good/fine" (Academic)
"Gottim" -> "I am good/fine" (Casual)

Formal and informal (in Shanti)

The only difference is that you, again, drop the pronoun and turn the standard SOV word order to
an "Object after verb" order. Basically SVO or VO, depending on if the subject is needed.

"Mun heja užan" -> "I am good/fine" (formal)
"Užan heja" -> "Am good/fine" (informal)

The most famous idiom in Diberian

If you are using the Affected auxiliary verb instead of the normal Unaffected auxiliary verb for sentences as "I am",
you get something on the line as:
- Gje iregan (From: Gire + giregan) -> "I can change" (Idiomatic formulation)
- Tje uresa (From: Sure + suresa) -> "You can change yourself"
- Fje ärðat (From: Farð + farðat) -> "He/She can change him/herself"
- Gjo urrad (From: Gor + gorrad) -> "We can change ourselves"
- Sje ecþe (From: Svecd + svecþé) -> "You all can change yourselves"
- Hja iðæ (From: Haiðe + haiðæ) -> "They can change themselves"

It can also be used by pairing as in "Sure giregan" -> "I would like to be able to change/affect you" (In an idiomatic expression).

Diberian

Diberian (endonym: ðyska) is a speculatively reconstructed language, which is proposed to have been spoken by the Diberians.
Diberians relatives are fairly unknown, yet the reconstruction proposes that Diberian is a relative of Basque.
Furthermore, it is related to Etruscan, Raetic and Iberians.

The reconstruction proposes that Basque, Iberian, Etruscan and the Raetic language all belong to a larger "Pre-Indo-European" language family.
More realistically: It proposes that Basque and the Tyrsenian language family share a common ancestor.
Iberian is a tough one, but it is often included in the proposed pre-Indo-European language family.

#MyOwnSpeculation
Proposed Evidence:
When we take a look at the pronoun "I" in each of the four languages:
- Ni (Basque) / Nik (Proto-Basque)
- Mi (Etruscan)
- Mi (Uncertain and debated Iberian pronoun)
- ? (Unknown Raetic)

And when we add the Diberian "ñgé", then it is, for me personally, far too suspicious to wave it away as a coincidence.

Let us have another look at basic nouns, such as Father:
- Aita (Basque)
- Apa (Etruscan)

But now look what happens if we add "Mother":
- Ama (Basque)
- Ati (Etruscan)

It seems that Etruscan "Ati" and Basque "Aita" are suspiciously similar. Now I have to acknowledge that one means "Mother"
and the other "Father", yet what if that happened because of a semantic change?

Therefore I come to the conclusion that the Vasconic, Tyrsenian and the Celtic substrate languages are descended from a common Paleo-European ancestor.

In any event, this is only a theory, especially because all of the languages above are poorly known. So maybe this can be seen as a kind of gap filler too.

Nydiska

Nydiska is simply a descendant of Diberian, which adopted Germanic vocabulary and underwent significant simplifications in grammar.
There isn't much more to say about it...

Shanti

Shanti is a Uralic language of its own proposed branch. It is completely based on Uralic languages such as Finnish, Estonian, Hungarian but also
Khanty. It is proposed that the Diberian people adopted this language as a religious or ritualistic language as they
expanded east towards the Ural Mountains. Yet it has undergone grammatical changes
as it was adopted by the Diberians.
