Diberian Culture

The Diberian Culture and espacially the "Diberian Languages" are a Speculative Linguistic Reconstruction project.

About

The Diberians are a Central-, Western- and Northern European ethnic group, characterised by the Diberian languages, a common culture, shared genetic ancestry, and are speculated to be among the last remaining direct descendants of Neolithic European populations in Europe.

Etymology

The word "Diberian" is taken from "Iberian" which typically refers to the Iberian Peninsula. Since "Diberians" are a non-Indo-European culture from North-East of the Rhine (more to this later), the term "Iberians" was mixed with the German "Deutsch" creating a Semantic change to refer to this culture. The native word "Ðyska" or "Dhyska" in standardized writing, comes from the actual self-description of the Diberian people, simply meaning: "People" or "Group of people".

History (Of Europe and Diberians)

~43.000 BCE - ~40.000 BCE

Groups of Homo sapiens arrived from regions to the southeast, ultimately descended
from populations that evolved in Africa. They moved through a landscape that was much colder than today and lived by hunting, fishing, and gathering
wild plants. They were not Europeans in the modern national or ethnic sense. They were small, mobile grouos without countries or written language.
Evidence suggests modern humans were
present in Europe by around 43.000BCE, with the timing varying by region.

Neanderthals had lived in Europe for hundreds of thousands of years. As modern humans
expanded, the two populations overlapped, sometimes interbred, and shared parts of the same world. Neanderthals disappeared as a distinct population
around 38.000BCE, although the timing varied geographically. Climate change, small population sizes, competition, and
interactions with modern humans may all have contributed. Many living people outside Africa carry some Neanderthal ancestry.

~40.000 BCE - ~20.000 BCE

People developed varied stone and bone tools, made clothing, used shelters, and created ornaments and figurative art.
Famous cave art sites include Chauvet Cave and, later,
Lascaux. Communities exchanged materials over considerable distances. Their
lives depended heavily on seasonal animal movements, plant availability, cooperation, and knowledge of local environments.

~26.000 BCE - ~19.000 BCE

Ice sheets covered much of northern Europe, glaciers and cold conditions made large areas difficult or impossible to inhabit.
People concentrated in more hospitable regions, including parts of southern and southeastern Europe.
Populations shifted and later expanded northward as conditions improved. The process was gradual and differed across regions.

As the proposed Proto-Language collapsed due to heavy migration, it began to form the "Pre-Indo-European" languages.

Each group settled in different places of Europe, some in southern France and northern Spain, some in southern Spain, others

~15.000 BCE - 10.000 BCE

As the last Ice Age ended, forests spread, coastlines changed, and reindeer-dominated
environments gave way to different ecosystems in many regions. Hunter-gatherers adapted their diets and
technologies and recolonized areas farther north. Sea levels rose substantially as ice melted, eventually seperating Britain
from continental Europe. People remained Hunter-gatherers for thousands of years after the climate warmed.

~7.000 BCE - ~4.500 BCE

Farming had developed earlier in the Fertile Crescent, in southwest Asia. Farming communities from Anatolia and neighboring regions
spread into southeastern Europe, bringing crops, domesticated animals, pottery traditions, and more settled ways of life. These newcomers
mixed to varying degrees with existing Hunter-gatherers. Farming spread through migration as well as the adoption of new practices by local people.

Farming villages expanded through much of continental Europe. People built longhouses, grew cereals, raised livestock, made pottery,
and cleared woodland for fields.
Food production supported larger populations, but it also brought new problems: infectious disease, crop failure, harder physical labor,
and greater dependence on harvests. Hunter-gatherer communities persisted in some areas.

~4.500 BCE - ~2.500 BCE

Communities in parts of Europe built megalithic tombs, stone circles, and other monuments. These structures required planning,
skilled labor, and cooperation across groups.
Wealth differences and social leadership became more visible in some societies. However, Europe's communities remained diverse:
there was no single European civilization or uniform way of life.

~3.300 BCE - ~2.500 BCE

Pastoralist populations associated with the Eurasian steppe moved into parts of Europe. Ancient DNA shows that these movements
caused major ancestry changes in many regions, particularly in central and northern Europe.
These people brought or helped spread pastoral traditions and probably contributed substantially to the spread of Indo-European languages
across much of Europe. The precise relationship between migration, language, and cultural change remains debated.
